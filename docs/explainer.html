Chapter 1 · The raw material

One row of data is one hour of oil

Your project watches WTI crude oil futures, the main US oil price. Every hour the market produces a bar: the open, high, low and close price for that hour, plus the volume traded. Over 2.4 years that gives 13,556 hourly bars. That table is the starting point for everything else.

Chapter 2 · Indicators

Turning prices into signals

Raw prices are hard to read, so traders compute indicators, simple formulas on past prices. The most important one in your project is the EMA (exponential moving average): an average that gives more weight to recent hours. If the price is above its EMA, the trend is “up”.

Chapter 3 · Many markets, one score

The original dashboard is a weighted vote

Oil doesn’t move alone. Your dashboard also watches the US dollar (oil is priced in dollars), refinery margins (the crack spread), the Brent–WTI gap, stocks (risk appetite) and oil volatility (OVX). Each becomes a “pillar” scored from −100 (bearish) to +100 (bullish). The final score is a weighted sum of the pillars, with weights a person chose by hand.

Chapter 4 · What “learning” means

A model learns by adjusting weights to reduce its mistakes

Each dot is one example: its position left–right is the score, and it sits at the top if price went up afterwards or the bottom if it went down. Logistic regression draws an S-shaped curve giving P(up) for any score. The curve’s steepness is the weight and its shift is the bias. Training means nudging both, step by step, to reduce the error (log-loss). That process is called gradient descent.

Chapter 5 · No peeking at the future

Train on the past, test on the future, then move forward

The biggest mistake in financial ML is letting the model see the future by accident (look-ahead bias). If you shuffle the hours randomly into training and test sets, the model trains on hours that come after the ones it is tested on. Your project uses walk-forward validation instead, plus a small purge gap so that no training answer overlaps the test period.

Remember Your settings: at least 2,000 hours of training, retrain every 250 hours, purge gap of 4 hours. Two sanity checks prove the scoring is honest: a model that cheats on purpose scores 100 %, a coin scores about 50 %.
Chapter 6 · Is 51 % good?

Luck can look like skill, unless you count carefully

If a coin “predicts” oil, it is right about half the time, but never exactly 50 %. With a few hundred calls, a lucky coin can reach 53 %. A result only means something if it lands outside the range a coin produces. That’s what a p-value measures. Your project also uses the Pesaran–Timmermann test, which corrects for oil rising slightly more often than falling.

About 2,000 independent 4-hour calls is roughly what your evaluation used.

Each grey dot is one fair coin. The shaded band holds 95 % of coin results.

Remember Your models (49.1 %–51.3 % at 4 hours) sit inside the coin band. That is why the honest conclusion is “no reliable edge”, even though 51.3 % sounds better than 50 %.
Chapter 7 · Honest probabilities

Where ML clearly helped: calibration

The old dashboard showed “bullish %” as if it were a probability. A probability is calibrated if “70 %” comes true about 70 % of the time. Using your backtest, the project checked what really happened after each reading, then trained a tiny logistic regression (Platt scaling) to map the score to an honest probability. Chapter 8 · A model that keeps learning

The Hedge algorithm re-weights the pillars after every outcome

Each pillar is treated as an expert voting on the next 4 hours. When an outcome arrives, every expert’s weight is multiplied by e−η·loss: right experts keep their weight, wrong ones shrink. The catch is delayed feedback: a 4-hour call can only be scored 4 hours later, so the model always learns from calls made 4 steps ago. A small fixed share (1 %) pulls every weight back towards equal each step, so no pillar can ever be switched off. Below it runs on 600 real consecutive hours from your lockbox period.

Press Play. Then turn the fixed share off and use η = 1.0: the weight piles onto the dollar and stock pillars, the two that almost always vote near zero.

Remember Every pillar is right about 49–50 % of the time on direction, so none is truly accurate. A cautious vote near zero costs less than a bold vote that is wrong half the time, so Hedge moves weight from the bold pillars (trend 26 % → 17 %) to the cautious ones (dollar and stocks 11 % → 18 %). Without the fixed share, nearly all weight goes to dollar and stocks. In every setting accuracy stays at chance level: learning cannot create a signal that isn’t in the data.
