60-second pitch

Memorise this I built PetroSync, a live crude-oil dashboard that turns nine markets into trading signals. I then made it a supervised machine-learning problem: will WTI be higher in four hours? I trained logistic regression, gradient boosting and a self-learning Hedge model on 13,436 hourly decisions, using walk-forward validation so no model ever saw the future. The honest result is that nothing predicts hourly direction better than chance, which matches the research on oil. But ML clearly improved the product: calibration fixed misleading probabilities, an ML filter improved trades, and the self-learning model showed which hand-tuned weights were unjustified. Most usefully, while the direction of oil is unpredictable, the size of its next move is predictable: my volatility model sorts calm from stormy hours correctly 69 % of the time, and it runs live. It all runs live, and a pre-registered forward test is checking the best candidates on future data.

Introduction

The problem. Crude oil is one of the most traded commodities. Its price reacts every hour to supply, demand, the US dollar, refinery margins and fear (volatility). Traders use dashboards that show signals and "win probabilities", but those numbers are rarely checked.

What the project is, in three parts:

  1. A dashboard (Flask + Chart.js) that downloads 9 markets hourly and computes 20+ indicators, a sentiment score (−100 to +100) and trade setups.
  2. An ML layer that turns those signals into features (31 of them) and learns to predict the 4-hour direction, calibrate probabilities, filter trades and re-weight itself.
  3. A strict evaluation that replays the system hour by hour on 2.4 years of history without look-ahead, plus a live forward test.
Be upfront The original dashboard is rule-based (hand-chosen weights). Say so before anyone asks. ML is what learns from it, is compared against it, and now runs inside it.

Research gaps

What the literature says (one line each)

  • Brock, Lakonishok & LeBaron (1992): simple moving-average rules showed predictive power on 90 years of stock data.
  • Marshall, Cahan & Cahan (2008): on 15 commodity futures, profits from ~7,800 technical rules disappear once you correct for testing so many rules (data snooping).
  • Alquist, Kilian & Vigfusson (2013): for oil, the “no change” forecast is very hard to beat.
  • Bailey et al. (2014): backtests tuned on history often just fit noise (backtest overfitting).

The gaps you address

  • G1 Trading tools report accuracy on the same data they were tuned on.
  • G2 The cited studies work at daily or monthly frequency, not hourly with intermarket data.
  • G3 Hand-tuned rules are rarely compared head-to-head with ML on the same features.
  • G4 Probabilities shown to traders are rarely checked for calibration, and few projects pre-register a live test.
Careful Don’t claim “nobody has ever studied hourly oil”. Say “the studies I reviewed work at daily or monthly frequency”.

  • Scale-free distances: divided by ATR (average true range), so a $1 move means the same at $60 or $110 oil.
  • Standardisation: StandardScaler (mean 0, std 1) for linear models, whose coefficients depend on scale. Trees don’t need it.
  • Rolling z-scores (improvement experiment): (value − mean of last 500 bars) ÷ std, using only past bars, so levels that drift over time stay comparable.
  • Cyclical hour: sin and cos of the hour, so the model knows 23:00 is next to 00:00.

Data preparation

Label: y = 1 if WTI’s close 4 hourly bars later is higher than now, else 0. 51.3 % of rows are “up”, so the classes are balanced and need no resampling. 1h and 24h labels were also tested.

Point-in-time rows (13,436): for every hour of history, the exact live engine function (compute_snapshot) is re-run on data cut off at that bar’s close. The replay matched the live dashboard (−24.9 vs −24.8).

3-class view: up / flat / down, where “flat” = move smaller than 0.25 × ATR × √h. Tiny moves aren’t scored as hits.

Methodology

The pipeline (ML steps highlighted)

Walk-forward validation

Train on everything before a point, test on the next block, move forward, repeat. Minimum training data 2,000 rows, retrain every 250 rows (~2 weeks), with a purge gap equal to the label horizon (4 bars; 30 for trade labels). The purge removes training rows whose 4-hour answer overlaps the test block.

Why not normal K-fold cross-validation? Random folds put future hours in the training set, which leaks the answer and inflates accuracy (Bergmeir & Benítez 2012; López de Prado 2018).

Baselines and sanity checks: always “up”, last-move persistence, a random coin (should score ~50 %), and an oracle that cheats on purpose (must score 100 %, which proves the scoring code is correct). p-values use non-overlapping samples, because overlapping 4-hour windows are not independent.

Self-learning model (online learning)

Idea: each of the 6 sentiment pillars is an “expert” voting on the 4-hour direction (its value ÷ 100, from −1 to +1). The Hedge algorithm (Freund & Schapire, 1997) changes their weights after every outcome.

loss_i = (vote_i − outcome)² / 4 outcome = +1 if price rose, −1 if it fell w_i ← w_i · exp(−η · loss_i) η = 0.01 w ← w / Σw (normalise) w ← (1 − α)·w + α/6 α = 0.01 “fixed share” score = 100 · Σ w_i · vote_i (same −100…+100 scale as the engine)
  • Delayed feedback: a 4-hour call can only be scored 4 hours later, so the model always uses outcomes that already exist.
  • Test-then-train evaluation (prequential): each hour the model predicts first, is scored, and only then learns.
  • Fixed share α: keeps every pillar at a minimum weight, so the model can recover if a pillar becomes useful again when the market changes.
  • η chosen on development data from {0.01, 0.05, 0.2, 0.5}. A small η suits noisy data: the model changes its mind slowly.
  • Live: it starts from the weights learned over 13,432 historical updates and keeps updating at every hourly refresh. The dashboard shows hand-tuned vs learned weights, plus a weight-history chart.
  • Second learner: online logistic regression (SGD) on all 31 features, taking one gradient step per newly labelled hour.

Why the weights moved: every pillar’s direction hit rate is 49–50 % (best: Brent–WTI 50.4 %), so none is truly accurate. Under the squared loss, a vote near 0 always costs about 0.25, while a bold vote that is right only half the time costs more on average. The dollar and stock pillars are almost always near zero (average |vote| 0.04–0.06), so Hedge rewards them for caution. Fixed share matters: with α = 0.01 the weights stay near equal (table above); with α = 0, about 97 % of the weight goes to dollar and stocks. Accuracy is at chance level in every setting.

What to say “The self-learning model moved weight away from the bold pillars, trend, VWAP and crack margin, towards the cautious ones, the dollar and stocks. That happened because no pillar is right more than about 50 % of the time on direction, so confident votes cost more than cautious ones. Accuracy stayed at chance level, 50.9 % in the lockbox, because learning can’t create a signal the data doesn’t contain.”
Why not 5-minute data or hourly retraining of everything? 5-minute moves are even noisier, Yahoo’s crude data is delayed ~10 minutes, and 5-minute history only covers 60 days. Retraining big models every hour adds one row to 13,000, which changes almost nothing and mostly chases noise.

Volatility forecasting (the strongest result)

Idea: big moves are followed by big moves and quiet hours by quiet hours (volatility clustering; Engle 1982, Bollerslev 1986). So the size of the next move is predictable even though its direction is not.

Target: realised volatility over the next 4 (and 24) hourly bars = √(average of squared hourly returns), modelled on a log scale.

Model: HAR (Corsi 2009, “heterogeneous autoregressive”): ridge regression (α = 10, standardised) on log realised volatility over the last 1, 4, 24 and 96 bars (hour, half-day, day, week), plus log OVX (the market’s own implied volatility) and hour of day (sine/cosine). Trained walk-forward like everything else.

  • Simple beats complex: HAR with 7 inputs matched gradient boosting with 38. Volatility mostly depends on recent volatility, so extra features add noise.
  • Honest ranges: a normal distribution promises 68 % inside ±1σ; oil’s fat tails give 61 % at 4 h. The dashboard shows the measured 61 %.
  • Does volatility help direction? No. Adding it to the direction model gave 51.4 %, AUC 0.506 (lockbox). Direction in “stormy” hours looked better in the lockbox (53.5 %) but not in development (50.9 %), so it is noise.
  • Live: the dashboard card shows the expected 4-hour move in dollars, the likely price range and the regime (very calm → very stormy). The live inputs were checked to match the backtest exactly.
  • Use: sizing positions and stop-losses, not choosing long or short.
What to say “You can’t predict which way oil goes, but you can predict how much it will move. The direction autocorrelation is −0.008, but the size autocorrelation is 0.22. A simple HAR model sorts calm from stormy 4-hour periods correctly 69 % of the time out of sample, and it runs live on the dashboard.”

ML ranks slightly better than the rules but is not statistically better than chance. Price forecasts: Theil U2 = 1.01–1.05, no better than “price stays the same”. A trap that was caught: at 1h, logistic regression looked significant (p = 0.007) only because it said “up” 72 % of the time; the drift-corrected test gives p = 0.08.

2 · Calibration (the clear ML win, deployed)

Old “bullish %”: Brier skill −0.079 (worse than guessing), calibration error 0.12. It showed 27–75 % bullish, but price rose about 50 % of the time in every band. After Platt calibration: Brier skill −0.001 and calibration error 0.01, in both development and lockbox periods. The dashboard now shows “calibrated P(up, next 4h)”, which stays between 50 % and 52.4 %.

3 · Trade setups: claimed vs real

Over 2,114 simulated trades: 45.8 % real vs 71.2 % claimed (p < 0.0001), profit factor 0.84, −$46 per trade after costs. Worst: value-area rotation, 19.7 % real vs 64 % claimed.

4 · Improvement experiment (38 variants, tuned on development, tested once on lockbox)

  • Confident ML calls only (top 30 %): 54.4 % → 54.7 %, significant in both periods. Promising, not proven.
  • Wider stop (1.5×ATR, 1.5R target) + ML trade filter: −$65 → −$6 per trade (dev), +$7 → +$98 (lockbox). Not yet significant.
  • Win rate ≠ profit: moving the target closer raised the win rate to 66 % without raising profit.

5 · Self-learning

Hedge: 50.9 % lockbox hit (= frozen engine). Online logistic regression: 51.5 %, AUC 0.508. Not significant. The value is in the weights it learned.

6 · Volatility (the clear win)

HAR forecast of the next 4 hours’ volatility: lockbox R² 0.22, calm vs stormy correct 68.7 % (AUC 0.70), 61 % of moves inside the ±1σ range. Direction stays unpredictable, but the size of the move is not.

Conclusion

Worked: volatility is predictable (68.7 % calm vs stormy, live on the dashboard); calibration fixed misleading probabilities (deployed); an ML trade filter improved results in both periods; self-learning weights run live and exposed unjustified hand-tuned weights; the evaluation catches look-ahead bias and drift.

Didn’t: no model predicts hourly direction better than chance; price forecasts didn’t beat “no change”; claimed win rates were 17–44 points too high.

Limitations: roll gaps in continuous futures; 1h bars hide the order of high and low within an hour; real 15m data covers only 60 days; one 2.4-year market regime.

Future work: finish the forward test (Feb 2027); daily horizon, where trend effects are documented; LSTM baseline under the same validation.

Glossary in plain words

EMA (exponential moving average)
Average price that weights recent bars more. Price above EMA = uptrend.
RSI (14)
0–100 momentum gauge; above 70 overbought, below 30 oversold.
ATR (average true range)
Average size of a bar’s move, i.e. volatility in dollars.
VWAP
Average price weighted by volume, a “fair value” reference.
POC / VAH / VAL
Price with the most volume; top and bottom of the zone holding 70 % of volume.
Crack spread (3:2:1)
Profit from refining 3 barrels of crude into 2 of gasoline and 1 of heating oil.
Brent–WTI spread
Price gap between global (seaborne) and US (landlocked) crude.
OVX
Implied volatility of oil, the oil “fear gauge”.
Supervised learning
Learning from examples with known answers (features → label).
Logistic regression
Linear model that outputs a probability via the sigmoid of Σ w·x.
Gradient boosting
Many small decision trees, each fixing the errors of the previous ones.
Regularisation (L2, C, α)
Penalty on large weights to stop the model memorising noise.
Overfitting
Fitting noise in the training data, so performance drops on new data.
Look-ahead bias / leakage
The model accidentally sees future information; accuracy looks fake-good.
Walk-forward validation
Train on the past, test on the next block, roll forward.
Purge gap
Training rows dropped so their labels don’t overlap the test period.
Lockbox
Held-out period scored once, after all choices are fixed.
Data snooping
Trying many variants and reporting the best; it finds luck.
Base rate
How often the event happens anyway (51.3 % up).
ROC-AUC
Probability that a random “up” case gets a higher score than a random “down” case.
Brier score
Mean squared error of probability forecasts; lower is better.
Calibration / Platt scaling
Mapping a score to how often the event actually happened, using logistic regression.
MCC
Correlation between predictions and truth; 0 = random, 1 = perfect.
Pesaran–Timmermann test
Significance test for direction forecasts that corrects for base rates.
p-value
Chance of a result at least this good if there were no real skill; < 0.05 is the usual bar.
Theil U2
Model error ÷ error of “no change”; below 1 beats the random walk.
Profit factor
Total winnings ÷ total losses; above 1 is profitable.
Meta-labeling
A second model that decides whether to take the first model’s trade.
Online learning
Updating a model one new example at a time instead of retraining from scratch.
Hedge / multiplicative weights
Experts’ weights are multiplied down by their losses after each round.
Prequential (test-then-train)
Predict, score, then learn, at every step.
Power analysis
How much data you need to detect an effect of a given size.
Pre-registration / SHA-256
Writing the test plan before data arrives; fingerprints prove the models weren’t changed.
